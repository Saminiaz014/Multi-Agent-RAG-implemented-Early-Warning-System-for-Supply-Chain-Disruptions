## SECTION 1 — Title / Framing Slide

**Suggested title:** EVAL01: Does the Multi-Agent Architecture Earn Its Complexity?

**Subtitle:** An offline, reproducible benchmark suite for the Strait-of-Hormuz early-warning DSS

**Speaker narrative:**
The production system (Phases 0–10) is a six-agent decision-support pipeline: shipping, market, geopolitical, routing, news, and disaster agents, aggregated by a weighted risk engine, explained by SHAP, and grounded by RAG retrieval of historical precedent. It is real, tested, and deployed — but it had never been benchmarked against the alternative: what if a supply-chain manager just used a control chart, or a data scientist just threw one Isolation Forest at all six signals? EVAL01 (built across eight sub-phases, R1–R8) answers that question directly, honestly, and reproducibly.

---

## SECTION 2 — The Problem That Motivated EVAL01

**Suggested title:** The Gap: Complexity Without Comparison

**Narrative / bullets:**
- The production pipeline had 108+ agent-level tests and thesis-grade evaluation metrics (Phase 9a), but every one of those numbers was measured *in isolation* — there was no side-by-side comparison against simpler alternatives.
- A thesis committee's obvious question: *"Why six agents and a weighted risk engine, instead of one off-the-shelf anomaly detector?"* — there was no evidence-backed answer.
- Three specific unanswered questions drove the design of EVAL01:
  1. **Floor question** — does the system even beat random guessing and trivial always/never-alarm rules?
  2. **Simplicity question** — does the system beat what a supply-chain manager already has today (a Z-score, an EWMA control chart, a CUSUM change detector)?
  3. **Complexity-justification question** — does the *specific* multi-agent design (six domains, tuned weights, a multi-domain agreement bonus) outperform a single, more powerful unsupervised model (Isolation Forest, Matrix Profile) trained on the same six signals?
- A fourth, quieter problem: existing synthetic data (Phase 1) was hand-built for the production pipeline's exact schema. It couldn't be reused for a controlled, labelled, multi-scenario benchmark without becoming a second parallel, undocumented data-generation path.

**Design constraint carried through every phase:** *no fabricated numbers, no cherry-picking, no comparison invented after the fact.* Every R-phase either measures something real or explicitly documents that it couldn't.

## SECTION 4 — R1: Region Registry + Scenario Spec Loader

**Suggested title:** R1 — Building an Honest Synthetic World

**Problem it solved:** Before EVAL01, "synthetic Hormuz data" meant one hardcoded generator built for the production pipeline. There was no way to define *multiple, comparably-structured disruption scenarios* — a severe crisis, a moderate one, a quiet baseline, and a deliberately deceptive one — without duplicating code for each.

**What it is:**
- `Region` — a YAML-driven spec describing a chokepoint (location, baseline traffic, which of the 6 domains are active there, whether traffic can reroute, how losses scale).
- `ScenarioSpec` + `materialize_scenario()` — turns a YAML scenario definition into a 365-day multi-domain synthetic dataset: correlated noise (AR(1)), seasonality, and per-domain event ramps with independent lead/lag timing, so a geopolitical signal can plausibly move *before* the physical shipping disruption while a market signal moves *after*.
- Four scenario classes were defined for Hormuz: a critical sustained crisis, a shorter high-severity event, a calm negative control, and a "decoy" negative control where one domain spikes (an unrelated news rumor) but nothing else does — deliberately designed to bait false alarms.

**How it strengthens the project:** Every later phase (R3–R8) depends on this being genuinely region-agnostic and data-driven — adding a second chokepoint later is a new YAML file, not new code. It also makes ground truth *by construction* — every day in every scenario has a known correct label, which is the entire precondition for benchmarking anything downstream.

## SECTION 5 — R2: Silent-Agent Handling in the Risk Engine

**Suggested title:** R2 — When an Agent Has Nothing to Say

**Problem it solved:** A weighted average of six agent scores breaks quietly when one agent is *enabled but has no real signal* — e.g. the geopolitical domain in a region with no geopolitical exposure. A naive average keeps that agent's near-zero score in the mix, permanently dragging the composite risk score down even when the other five agents are screaming.

**What it is:**
- `SilentAgentDetector` — flags an agent as silent using a simple, defensible empirical rule (its own score barely moves, mean or max stays below threshold).
- `WeightRenormalizer` — reallocates a silent agent's weight across the remaining active agents so the composite score reflects what the *live* agents are actually saying.
- Integrated into the risk engine (`aggregate_detections()`) without changing the shared `DetectionResult` data contract used everywhere else in the codebase — a deliberately minimal, backward-compatible change.

**How it strengthens the project:** This is a robustness property, not a performance one — it's what keeps the system usable when deployed to a new region where not every domain applies (a direct real-world scenario, not a hypothetical).

## SECTION 6 — R3: Scenario Batch Generator

**Suggested title:** R3 — Turning One Scenario Into a Reproducible Grid

**Problem it solved:** A benchmark needs many runs — the same scenario re-seeded multiple times — to say anything statistically meaningful. Re-implementing the R1 generator to do this would violate the project's own "don't duplicate logic" principle and risk subtle drift between the two paths.

**What it is:** `ScenarioBatchGenerator` is a *thin wrapper* around R1's `materialize_scenario()` — it never reimplements signal generation. It runs the 4 scenario classes across 5 seeds each (20 runs), adds two convenience integer-coded label columns, and writes one parquet file per run.

**How it strengthens the project:** This is the dataset every subsequent baseline and ablation is scored against — its correctness (or a bug in it) would silently invalidate every downstream number. Keeping it a thin, auditable wrapper rather than a second implementation is what makes that trustworthy.

## SECTION 7 — R4: Tier 0 — Control Baselines

**Suggested title:** R4 — Establishing the Floor and the Ceiling

**Problem it solved:** A results table with no reference point is meaningless — "F1 = 0.65" means nothing without knowing what pure chance, or a detector that never shuts up, or one that never speaks up, would score on the same data.

**What it is:**
- **Random** — uniform random anomaly score, seeded for reproducibility.
- **Always-alarm** — constant maximum score (the recall ceiling, precision floor).
- **Never-alarm** — constant zero score (the inverse).
- `BaselineEvaluator` — the shared metric engine every later baseline reuses: AUC-PR, AUC-ROC, F1 at a pre-declared threshold, best-achievable F1, precision, recall, false-positive rate, macro-F1 — all computed on a held-out test split after the threshold itself is chosen on a separate validation split (no peeking).
- Two metric families (VUS-PR/ROC, and five event/range/point-adjusted F1 variants) were deliberately **not implemented** here rather than approximated — they need a library (`tslearn`) or evaluation logic this project doesn't have yet, and every later result table reports them as an explicit, explained gap rather than a silent omission.

**How it strengthens the project:** Every number reported for the "real" baselines and ablations in R5–R8 is now interpretable *relative to* this floor/ceiling — a critical piece of evaluation honesty.

**Suggested visuals:**
- `chart_A_baseline_auc_pr.png` — all 10 baselines (the 3 controls from this phase plus every later baseline) ranked by AUC-PR, mean ± std error bars.
- `chart_B_baseline_fpr.png` — the same 10, ranked by false-positive rate (lower is better) — pairs well side-by-side with chart A since AUC-PR and FPR are deliberately not combined on one axis.
- **Table:** `results/results_by_baseline.csv` — the full mean ± std table behind both charts (8 metrics × 10 baselines).

## SECTION 8 — R5: Tier 1 — Statistical / SPC Baselines

**Suggested title:** R5 — What a Supply-Chain Manager Already Has

**Problem it solved:** The most credible critique of any new detection system is "a spreadsheet with a control chart would have caught this too." R5 tests that claim directly, on the single signal (shipping volume) a manager would actually be watching.

**What it is:** Five single-signal baselines: **Z-score**, **EWMA** control chart (its smoothing parameter tuned on held-out data, not guessed), **CUSUM** change detector (its alarm threshold likewise tuned), **SARIMA** residual thresholding, and a naive **persistence** ("today looks like yesterday's error") baseline.

**Key finding (a real one, not a smoothed-over prediction):** CUSUM is genuinely competitive on a large, sustained disruption — once it triggers on the big shock, it stays pinned at alarm for the rest of the evaluation window rather than fading back to quiet, giving it perfect recall on that scenario. **This is honest evidence that classical change detection is good at exactly what it's built for** — the case for the multi-agent system has to rest on multi-domain fusion and explanation, not on being faster at spotting one clean signal move.

**Suggested visual:** `chart_E_cusum_stickiness.png` — CUSUM's actual daily score across the full year on `hormuz_P_CRIT`: noisy before the disruption, then pinned flat at maximum alarm from the shock onward, straight through the held-out test split. This single line *is* the "recall = 1.0, it never comes back down" finding — one of the strongest visuals in the deck.

## SECTION 9 — R6: Tier 2 — Classical Unsupervised Baselines

**Suggested title:** R6 — Why Not Just One Machine-Learning Model?

**Problem it solved:** The single hardest question for a six-agent architecture: *why not train one strong unsupervised model on all six signals together, instead of building and maintaining six separate agents plus an aggregation layer?*

**What it is:**
- **Isolation Forest** — a multivariate ensemble trained on all six domain signals concatenated together.
- **Matrix Profile** (via `stumpy.mstump`) — genuine multi-dimensional shape-anomaly detection across all six signals jointly, not six separate univariate profiles glued together.

**Key findings (both confirmed empirically, not assumed):**
- Isolation Forest achieves the **strongest raw detection numbers of any baseline in the entire suite** on the real crisis scenario (AUC-ROC ≈ 0.99, F1 ≈ 0.81) — it genuinely beats the multi-agent approach on pure detection power.
- But on the "decoy" scenario — one domain spiking with no real disruption — Isolation Forest's raw anomaly score **nearly doubles** despite there being nothing to detect. It has no mechanism to require multi-domain agreement before raising an alarm. **This is precisely the false-alarm vulnerability the multi-agent system's consensus/agreement-bonus mechanism (tested in R7) exists to guard against.**

**Suggested visual:** `chart_D_iforest_decoy_false_alarm.png` — Isolation Forest's score on `hormuz_N_DECOY` across the full year: a thin line shows the genuinely noisy raw daily score, a bold line shows the 15-day rolling mean, which climbs from a 0.32 quiet-period baseline to 0.60 exactly during the single-domain news-rumor window. (Note for whoever builds the deck: the first draft of this chart used only the raw daily score, and the decoy bump didn't actually look visually distinct from other noise spikes elsewhere in the series — the rolling-mean overlay was added specifically so the chart shows what was actually measured, not an overstated version of it. Worth keeping that framing if this comes up in questions.)

## SECTION 10 — R7: Tier 5 — Ablations (A0–A7)

**Suggested title:** R7 — Does the Aggregation Strategy Itself Add Value?

**Problem it solved:** All the above answers "is a multi-domain approach good?" — R7 asks the sharper question: *given multiple domains, does the specific weighting-and-consensus strategy matter, or would any reasonable combination do?* Isolating this required a subtlety: the *production* agents need a rich, multi-column input schema that the synthetic benchmark data doesn't produce, so re-using them here would have meant fabricating input data. Instead, R7 uses lightweight, honest "domain scorers" (the same simple z-score method as R5) purely to isolate the *aggregation* question from the *agent-implementation-quality* question — documented in full in a dedicated rationale note.

**The eight configurations, in increasing sophistication:**

| Config | What changes | Question answered |
|---|---|---|
| A0 | 1 domain only | Floor: is a single signal enough? |
| A1 | 2 domains, fixed weights | Does adding a second domain help? |
| A2 | 4 domains, fixed weights | Does adding more domains help? |
| A3 | All 6 domains, equal weights | Does *any* combination beat one domain? |
| A4 | All 6, hand-tuned weights | Does expert judgment improve on equal weighting? |
| A5 | All 6, **automatically tuned weights** (a real optimization search, not a guess) | Does automated tuning beat hand-tuning? |
| A6 | A5's weights **+ a multi-domain agreement bonus** | **The key test** — does requiring several domains to agree before boosting the alarm reduce false alarms? |
| A7 | A6, with the explanation-retrieval step skipped | Confirms the explanation layer never changes the detection score itself |

**Honest result — reported as found, not adjusted to match the prediction:** the expected steady improvement from A0 through A6 did not hold, and A5 vs. A6 showed no measurable difference on the decoy scenario. Both outcomes were traced to real, specific causes and documented rather than hidden — a rolling-window statistical artifact that happens to land exactly on the evaluation boundary for several domains, and an evaluation window that (for that one scenario) contains no positive-labelled days at all, so the consensus mechanism was never actually put to the test there. **This is presented as a benchmark-design lesson, not a failure of the underlying idea** — and it's exactly the kind of finding that makes a thesis defense stronger, not weaker: it shows the evaluation is real and adversarial to its own hypothesis, not tuned to produce a nice story.

**Suggested visual:** `chart_C_ablation_progression.png` — a bar per config (A0–A7), blue for the domain-count/weighting configs (A0–A4), orange for the tuned + consensus configs (A5–A7), with the actual (non-monotonic) result plotted plainly and the caveat printed directly on the chart. This is the single most important chart for the "we report what we find, not what we expected" argument — put it up before anyone asks why the numbers don't climb. **Table:** `results/ablation_findings.csv`.

## SECTION 11 — R8: Metrics Aggregation & Honest Results Reporting

**Suggested title:** R8 — Reporting Only What Was Actually Measured

**Problem it solved:** Aggregating 232 separate result files into thesis-ready tables is mechanical — but the real risk at this stage is *narrative inflation*: filling gaps in the data with plausible-sounding placeholder numbers, or presenting an untested capability comparison as if it were measured. R8 was built specifically to resist that.

**What it is:** Two scripts that (1) load every result file from R4–R7, fix two real data-extraction bugs found along the way, and produce clean summary tables; (2) generate a markdown narrative where **every single number is read live from those tables** — there is no pre-written target to compare against, no capability checklist, no "full system" score, because none of those things exist anywhere in the project to honestly report. The summary explicitly lists every metric family that was *not* measured, with the specific reason why and what would be needed to add it.

**How it strengthens the project (and the thesis defense):** This is the section that pre-empts the hardest committee questions — "what about metric X?", "did you compare against Y?" — by answering them directly, in writing, before they're asked, rather than being caught improvising an answer live.

**Tables produced by this phase (all four are candidates for an appendix/backup slide):**
- `results/results_all_runs.csv` — every one of the 232 individual scored runs, unaggregated.
- `results/results_by_baseline.csv` — mean ± std per baseline (source of charts A and B).
- `results/results_by_scenario.csv` — best-F1 per baseline per scenario type.
- `results/ablation_findings.csv` — the A0–A7 comparison (source of chart C).

## SECTION 12 — Key Findings ("Results Gathered") — Consolidated

**Suggested title:** What EVAL01 Actually Found

1. **Classical change detection (CUSUM) is a real, competitive baseline** on a single sustained disruption — the case for multi-agent complexity is fusion and explainability, not raw single-signal detection speed.
2. **A single multivariate ML model (Isolation Forest) beats the aggregation baselines on raw detection metrics for a genuine event** — but is measurably more prone to false alarms on single-domain noise, exactly the failure mode multi-domain consensus is meant to prevent.
3. **Silent-agent handling and threshold pre-declaration are not academic details** — without them, a naive weighted average or a leaked threshold would have made every other result in this suite unreliable.
4. **A rolling-window statistical artifact at the evaluation boundary** made every multi-domain aggregation configuration score *worse* than a single clean signal on one scenario — a genuine, traced, documented limitation of the current domain-scoring method, not of the underlying multi-agent idea.
5. **A consensus mechanism can't be validated on a scenario with no positive days in its test window** — a concrete, actionable lesson for how EVAL01's own evaluation windows need to be redesigned going forward.
6. **Along the way, real bugs were found and fixed** in the evaluation pipeline itself (a regex matching the wrong separator character, a metric being silently read from the wrong field, missing-data handling that could have silently zeroed out entire statistics, a threshold-search boundary condition, an incorrect constructor argument) — evidence that the benchmark was actively exercised and debugged against real data, not written once and assumed correct.
7. **The same scrutiny was applied to how results are *shown*, not just how they're computed** — the first draft of the Isolation Forest decoy chart (raw daily score only) technically used real numbers but visually overstated the finding, since the series is noisy everywhere and the decoy bump didn't stand out from ordinary noise. It was redone with a rolling-mean overlay so the chart shows the actual windowed-mean effect that was measured. Small detail, but it's evidence the "no invented numbers" discipline extended to visualization choices, not just the underlying statistics.

## SECTION 13 — Honest Limitations (What EVAL01 Does Not Claim)

**Suggested title:** What This Benchmark Does Not Test

- **Not the production agents.** Ablations (R7) use lightweight scoring proxies, not `ShippingAgent`/`MarketAgent`/etc., because the production agents require a richer per-domain data schema than the synthetic benchmark currently generates.
- **Not the full system.** No single run in this suite combines detection with SHAP explanation, RAG retrieval, and decision recommendation — those live in the production pipeline and are untouched here.
- **Not real-world data.** All 20 scenarios are synthetic, grounded in documented disruption shapes but not validated against live historical events.
- **Not operational performance.** Alert fatigue, cost-weighted decision quality, and deployment latency are out of scope.
- **Several metric families are honestly unavailable**, not silently skipped: two need an unadded dependency (`tslearn`), several need segment-level evaluation logic not yet built, one needs onset-detection logic that doesn't exist yet, and one needs decision-label ground truth beyond what the scenarios currently generate.

## SECTION 14 — What's Next (Future Work)

**Suggested title:** Roadmap: From Benchmark to Full Validation

1. **Close the production-agent gap.** Extend the synthetic scenario generator (R1/R3) to produce the richer, multi-column schema the real agents need, so the ablation study can eventually run against the actual production agents instead of proxies.
2. **Implement the missing metric families.** Add `tslearn` for VUS-PR/VUS-ROC (the two metrics most directly comparable to published TSB-AD benchmark leaderboards), and build the segment-level (event-based, range-based, point-adjusted) evaluation logic the current suite explicitly flags as missing.
3. **Add a genuine lead-time metric.** Build an onset-detection evaluator that measures *how many days early* each method raises a real alarm — directly relevant to the "early warning" claim in the system's own name.
4. **Full 5-seed ablation run.** R7 currently runs the ablation grid on a single seed for speed; a full 5-seed run (matching the rigor already applied to R4–R6) would let ablation findings be reported with the same mean ± std confidence as the baseline comparisons.
5. **Fix the rolling-window evaluation-boundary artifact** identified in R7 — either by redesigning the domain-scorer normalization or by choosing evaluation windows that don't straddle a recent event peak.
6. **Redesign evaluation windows for negative-control scenarios** so a consensus/agreement mechanism can actually be exercised and measured on them, closing the gap identified in Section 12, finding 5.
7. **Full-system integration benchmark.** Extend EVAL01 to score the complete pipeline — detection *and* SHAP explanation *and* RAG-retrieved precedent *and* the final action recommendation — as a single end-to-end evaluation, not detection alone.
8. **Multi-region extension.** The region/scenario system (R1) was explicitly built to be region-agnostic; the next natural extension is adding scenario specs for the other chokepoints already referenced in the production system's live-extraction configuration (Bab el-Mandeb, Malacca, Suez), turning this into a genuine multi-region benchmark.
9. **Real-world validation.** Once the above are in place, validate the benchmark's findings against actual historical disruption episodes rather than synthetic reconstructions of them.

## SECTION 15 — Closing / Thesis Framing Slide

**Suggested title:** Why This Matters for the Thesis

**Narrative:**
EVAL01 doesn't just add more evaluation numbers — it changes the shape of the argument. Before it, the thesis could describe *what* the system does. After it, the thesis can defend *why it's built this way*, with a documented, reproducible, and — critically — self-critical evidence trail: real baselines it does and doesn't beat, a real ablation study of its own design choices, and an explicit, honest account of what still isn't known. That combination — strong results where they exist, and precise, traced explanations where they don't — is a stronger thesis position than a suite of only-positive results would have been.
